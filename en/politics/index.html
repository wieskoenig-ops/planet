Two clocks

An essay on climate policy, tipping points and responsibility for processes that outlast a legislative term.

5 November 20267 min read

Translated from the German original.

IForty years

In 1979 scientists from all over the world met in Geneva for the first World Climate Conference. In 1988 the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change, the IPCC, was founded. In 1992, in Rio de Janeiro, almost every country on Earth signed the Framework Convention on Climate Change. The first Conference of the Parties took place in Berlin in 1995, followed by the Kyoto Protocol in 1997 and the Paris Agreement in 2015: keep warming well below two degrees, preferably at one and a half.

Since then the parties have met every year, in Copenhagen and Marrakesh, in Glasgow, Dubai, Baku and Belém. Soon there will have been thirty conferences. The physics behind it has been known for decades, the warnings are precise, the reports keep getting thicker.

And yet global CO₂ emissions from fossil fuels are around sixty per cent higher today than in 1990, the year to which all reduction targets refer. 2024 was the first calendar year in which the global mean temperature was more than one and a half degrees above the pre-industrial level.

This is not the failure of individuals. It is a finding about the way we make decisions.

IIThe logic of compromise

Almost two hundred states negotiate at the climate conferences. Decisions are taken by consensus; a voting rule could never be agreed. Every state can block, and so every text is watered down until nobody objects any more.

What remains in the end is the lowest common denominator. In Glasgow, at the last minute, the phase-out of coal became a phase-down. In Dubai the parties agreed to “transition away” from fossil fuels – with no date and no quantities. The Paris Agreement itself is binding in that every state must submit targets, but not in how ambitious they are or whether they are met.

Diplomatically this is an achievement. It is hard enough to get two hundred governments to the same table at all. Physically it is a problem. For the climate does not negotiate. It responds to quantities, not to wording.

IIITwo clocks

Politics measures time in legislative terms: four, five years. Within that time something must become visible that can be shown to voters. The costs of climate protection arise immediately – at the petrol pump, on the electricity bill, when a heating system is replaced. The benefits lie decades away and are invisible. A disaster that was prevented produces no pictures.

The climate clock measures in different units. A large share of the carbon dioxide emitted stays in the atmosphere for centuries, a remainder for millennia. Sea level keeps rising long after the temperature has stabilised. Ice sheets respond over centuries. What is decided today takes effect at a time when none of today's decision-makers will still be alive.

You do not have to accuse politicians of indifference to see where the problem lies. Many understand the danger very well. But the institutions they work in are built for a different kind of problem: problems you can see before they become irreversible.

One clock measures terms of office. The other measures millennia. And only one of them can be turned back.

IVThe missing word

Tipping points are much talked about these days. The term has made it into speeches, talk shows and government statements. But it is mostly used like a red line, like a speed limit: you should not cross it, and if you do, you simply slow down again.

Another word is almost entirely missing: hysteresis. It describes the fact that a system which has tipped does not tip back when the cause is removed. That the way back is different from the way there – much longer, much more expensive, sometimes non-existent.

How deeply linear thinking is rooted is shown by a term from climate scenarios: overshoot. It means temporarily exceeding one and a half degrees and later bringing the temperature down again with technology that removes CO₂. For the temperature that may be possible. For an ice sheet that has started to slide, for an ocean current that has shut down, for a dead coral reef, it is not. The Global Tipping Points Report concluded in 2025 that warm-water coral reefs have probably already crossed their tipping point.

On top of that, the consequences do not grow evenly with warming. Beyond a threshold, a tenth of a degree more does not mean a little more damage but a different state. And tipping points can trigger one another, like dominoes. Nonlinear systems do not reward waiting.

VGermany

Germany started early. In 1987 the Bundestag set up a commission of inquiry on protecting the Earth's atmosphere; in 1990 the federal government decided to cut CO₂ emissions by a quarter by 2005. The target was missed. Much of the early reduction came from the collapse of East German industry anyway.

Today greenhouse gas emissions are just under half below the 1990 level. Most electricity now comes from renewable sources. The Climate Protection Act provides for a cut of 65 per cent by 2030 and climate neutrality by 2045. In transport and buildings, however, there has been little progress.

In 2021 the Federal Constitutional Court ruled that the climate law then in force violated the fundamental freedoms of future generations: whoever does too little today forces even harsher cuts on those who come later. It is one of the few places where the state was expressly required to think beyond its own legislative term.

And then there is the sentence one often hears: Germany is so small, what difference can we make? Germany causes just under two per cent of global emissions. That sounds like little. But per head it is well above the world average, and historically it is among the largest emitters of all. Above all: a great many countries are at one or two per cent. If each of them reasons this way, nobody is responsible any more.

Nor is a country's influence measured only in tonnes. When Germany supported solar power with fixed feed-in tariffs in the early 2000s, it created a market that pushed mass production. Since then solar power has become around ninety per cent cheaper, and the whole world benefits from it today. A country can show that an industrial nation can cut its emissions without becoming poor. Or it can show that it cannot. Either way, it is being watched.

VIResponsibility

Politicians are not the only ones responsible. They are elected, and voters rarely reward hardship. But politicians bear a particular responsibility, because they are the only ones who can change the rules by which everyone else acts.

This responsibility begins with honesty. It means talking not only about targets but about irreversibility. Saying: some decisions cannot be corrected in the next term of office. It means creating institutions that think further ahead than a cabinet: binding budgets, independent councils, courts that take the interests of the unborn seriously.

In 1979, the year of the first World Climate Conference, the philosopher Hans Jonas formulated the thought that is missing here: if the consequences of our actions reach so far that they determine the living conditions of future people, then our responsibility must reach just as far. He called this the heuristics of fear: when in doubt, give more weight to the worse forecast than to the better one, because an error in one direction can be corrected and in the other it cannot.

This is not panic. It is the political translation of hysteresis.

VIICoda: Physics does not negotiate

At the conferences, delegates wrestle over wording, over commas, over deadlines. That is the craft of diplomacy, and it has its value.

But on the other side of the table sits no one. The ice, the ocean, the reefs make no concessions and grant no extensions. They respond to one thing only: what actually arrives in the atmosphere.

The political clock can be reset; every four years it starts again. The other clock runs in one direction only. The task of this generation of politicians is not to save the world. It is more modest and more difficult: to decide as if one knew which of the two clocks will be proved right in the end.
