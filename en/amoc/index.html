Climate

The conveyor belt dies quietly

An essay on the AMOC, the physics of irreversibility and the failure of linear thinking.

16 June 20267 min read

Translated from the German original.

IThe machine

Imagine a machine that nobody built.

It is older than any civilisation, older than the first tools, older than the first fires. It has no moving parts, no piston, no engine. It runs on density and temperature, on salt and gravity. And it is the reason why London is milder than Labrador, why Oslo knows grapes and Newfoundland does not, why northern Europe is habitable at all for two hundred million people.

Its name is AMOC – Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation. A name that sounds like a government agency. The machine itself is a marvel.

It works like this: warm, salty surface water travels north from the tropics. The Atlantic as a conveyor, as a channel, as a gigantic blood vessel of the planet. Arriving in the north, the water gives off its heat to the atmosphere. It cools. It becomes heavier. It sinks – slowly, inexorably, to depths of four thousand metres. And then it turns around, as a cold deep current, back to the south. There it rises again. And the cycle begins anew.

This circulation is not a metaphor. It is physical. It is real. In the past ten thousand years it has not stopped a single time.

Until now.

IIThe fingerprint

There is a patch in the North Atlantic, south of Greenland, that behaves like no other place on Earth.

While the world's oceans are heating up – everywhere, evenly, relentlessly – this patch is cooling. By almost one degree Celsius since 1900. A single place running the other way, an island of anomaly in an ocean of warming. Oceanographers call it the “North Atlantic warming hole” – although that is exactly what it is not. A cold spot in a heating world.

It is no coincidence. It is a symptom.

The cooling reaches down to four thousand metres. It is the visible fingerprint of a current that is faltering. For decades the AMOC has been carrying less heat northward than it used to. The cold water that once sank into the depths and flowed south stays at the surface for longer. It accumulates. It cools the patch.

Whoever sees this patch on a map sees the AMOC dying. Not metaphorically. Literally.

IIICritical slowing down

Systems theory has a concept called critical slowing down.

It describes the behaviour of complex systems shortly before they tip. A system approaching a turning point loses its ability to recover. It takes longer to respond to disturbances. It fluctuates more strongly. It becomes sluggish – not because it gives up, but because the feedback loops that keep it stable grow weaker.

An ecosystem shows critical slowing down before it collapses. A financial market before it crashes. A heart rhythm before it breaks.

The AMOC shows it too.

Measurements show that the current has become more restless over recent decades. More irregular. More sensitive. And at the southern boundary of the Atlantic, at 34 degrees south, a measurement regarded as one of the most important early warnings has reversed: the net freshwater transport no longer flows in the right direction. The system is losing its drive.

At the same time, Greenland is melting. The meltwater is light and fresh. It settles like a lid over the North Atlantic. And it blocks precisely what keeps the machine running: the sinking of heavy, salty water into the depths.

The machine is suffocating under a lid of meltwater.

The worst thing is not the collapse itself. It is what comes afterwards – or rather, what no longer comes.

IVThe law of hysteresis

Physics has a term for what would happen to the AMOC if it tipped: hysteresis. It is the law of the irreversible path. It says: the way back is entirely different from the way there.

A simple image: modelling clay. You can shape it into a ball. You can squash it flat. But you cannot get it back into the original ball simply by letting go. The way back exists, but it costs far more energy than the way there.

With the AMOC it is more radical. Once the current has collapsed, it no longer carries salt from the tropics to the north. The north stays permanently fresh. The water stays permanently light. The sinking stays permanently absent.

Even if humanity stopped all emissions tomorrow. Even if we lowered the CO₂ concentration in the atmosphere back to pre-industrial levels. Even then: the AMOC would not start up again.

The river has lost its bed.

To restart the current, the Earth would have to be artificially cooled far below its historical norm. The Atlantic would have to be actively salted. Such interventions lie beyond any realistic technological capacity we can imagine for the coming centuries.

There is no repair. There is only before and after.

V2055 to 2063

Older climate models said: the risk before 2100 is low. Reassuringly vague.

Newer models – high-resolution, refined by better ocean data and revised emission scenarios – say something different. Van Westen and Dijkstra (2025) analysed 25 climate models using a physics-based indicator – the surface buoyancy flux in the North Atlantic – and reached a result that no longer lies in the distant future: under a persistently high emission scenario (SSP5-8.5) the collapse begins statistically from 2055, under an intermediate scenario (SSP2-4.5) from 2063.

These are not science-fiction coordinates. This is within the lifetime of people who are children today.

The consequences of an AMOC collapse would not be evenly distributed. North-western Europe would cool by several degrees within decades – while the rest of the world kept heating up. European agriculture would shift or collapse. Sea levels on the US east coast would rise, because the Gulf Stream would no longer push the water masses northward. The monsoons would shift. Billions of people whose water supply and food security depend on stable climate patterns would be destabilised.

And all of it without a return ticket.

VIFlying blind

In Washington, the measuring network is being dismantled.

Through the National Science Foundation, the current US administration has ordered the dismantling of the Ocean Observatories Initiative – the network of deep-sea sensors that continuously delivers data from the Atlantic. The climate agency NOAA faces budget cuts of 1.6 billion dollars.

Scientists warn: if these data series break off, we lose not just measurements. We lose decades of comparability. Continuous time series are the most valuable thing in climate research – they are the only way to tell trends from noise. Once interrupted, they are lost for good.

Humanity is navigating towards a possible tipping event – and actively dismantling its instruments as it goes.

VIIThe failure of linear thinking

Here is the real question, the one that will not let go.

The data exist. The physics is understood. Intelligence services – in Iceland, in Scandinavia, at the German BND – classify the risk of a collapsing AMOC as a threat to national security. There are urgent scientific letters to governments. There are models. There are early warning signs.

And still nothing proportionate happens.

This is not down to ignorance. It is down to a deep structural mismatch between the physics of the AMOC and the logic of human institutions.

The physics of the AMOC is nonlinear. It knows tipping points, hysteresis, irreversible changes of state. It works on the principle: for a long time little happens. Then everything happens at once. And then it is too late.

The logic of legislative terms is linear. You invest, you see results, you declare success. Or you wait for the problem until it becomes visible – and then solve it. That has worked for most human problems. For nonlinearities it does not work. For hysteresis it works catastrophically.

There is no political mechanism that says: act today, because in thirty years a state could arise from which there is no way back. That is not a sentence that wins an election campaign. It is not a sentence for which anyone hires lobbyists. It is not a sentence that fits into a coalition agreement.

The hysteresis of the Earth and the hysteresis of politics are incompatible. And we know it.

VIIICoda: What remains

I come back to the machine that nobody built.

It has stabilised Europe's climate since the last ice age ended. It created the conditions under which agriculture, cities and cultures could arise. It was always there. Invisible, unnoticed, indispensable.

We never learned to see it. And now that we can see it – through satellites, through deep-sea sensors, through models and measurements – we are dismantling the instruments that show us how it is doing.

The AMOC is not dying dramatically. It is slowing down. It is withdrawing. It is giving up quietly, decade by decade, at a depth no human ever sees.

Whether it will tip, we do not know for certain. Models are models, not prophecies. There are still uncertainties, still debates, still windows.

But the windows are closing.

And somewhere, four thousand metres down, south of Greenland, a patch of ocean is cooling while the world around it burns.
