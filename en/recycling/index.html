A cascade, not a cycle

Five recycling rates between 6 and 69 per cent, and the question behind the 15 per cent of Antalya.

22 November 20269 min read

Translated from the German original.

As of Sunday, 22 November 2026, afternoon. COP31 did not adopt a closing text. This essay describes the presidency’s goal, not an outcome.

IA word that promises more than it measures

A circle has no losses. What goes round comes back, and comes back unchanged. The term “circular economy” carries this image with it, and that is exactly why it is so popular. Whoever hears it thinks of bottles that become bottles again.

The Turkish COP31 presidency turned it into a programme. For 2035 it names, among other things, the goal of raising the use of circular materials in industry to 15 per cent, and of halving the growth of global waste.

That sounds measurable. The question is what is being measured. Plastic shows this most clearly, and it shows it from the start: everyone who asks for the recycling rate gets a different number.

IIThere are numbers, but they do not fit together

One would think that after years of debate about plastic waste there would be a reliable figure. Instead there are five, and each is correct if you know its definition.

  • 6.9 per cent: the share of secondary material in everything that enters the world economy, most recently in the Circularity Gap Report, with a falling trend. This is a figure for all materials, not just plastic.
  • 9 per cent: the share of plastic waste that is actually recycled worldwide. That is the OECD calculation, though based on data from 2019.
  • Just over 10 per cent: the share of “circular” plastics in world production in 2024. Plastics Europe, the industry association, reports that the figure exceeded ten per cent for the first time (43.9 million tonnes). Total production rose at the same time to 430.9 million tonnes, 16.3 per cent more than in 2018.
  • About 50 per cent: the recycling rate for plastic packaging in Germany under the EU method, which counts the material after process losses (NABU, 2023 data).
  • 68.9 per cent: the same German rate, but under the method of the German Packaging Act, which measures at the input to the final recycling process, according to the Central Agency Packaging Register for 2023.

The two German figures describe the same country in the same year. The difference of almost 20 percentage points lies solely in where the measurement is taken: before or after the losses.

The consequence: a figure that starts from the plastic at the beginning of the process is larger than one that counts what comes out at the end. And none of these figures says how good the resulting material is. I have not found a global indicator for the quality of recycled plastic.

IIIWhere the circle ends

Mechanical recycling means: collect, sort, wash, shred, melt, mould again. The material suffers every time it is melted. A study in Nature Communications quantified the degradation of polyethylene during repeated reprocessing: heat and shear forces shorten or branch the molecular chains, and how much depends on the grade, the process and contamination. Even a little polypropylene in the polyethylene worsens its properties.

The consequence is familiar from practice. A bottle does not become a bottle, but fibre, film, garden furniture. This pattern is called downcycling, or a cascade. The figures of the Ellen MacArthur Foundation for 2013, as reported by Oceana, show it: of the 14 per cent of packaging plastic collected for recycling, only two percentage points ended up as products of equal or higher value. Eight were downgraded, four were lost in the process. The data are old; the principle is not.

One caveat belongs here, because it makes the text more honest. A research team at the University of Manchester warns against precisely these words: “downcycling” is poorly defined and carries a value judgement. A downgraded stream can yield a high-quality product, and “upcycling” can cost the environment more. What matters, they argue, is what the end product is actually worth and how it adds up in an assessment, not what it is called.

That is the right yardstick. It rules out the buzzword downcycling just as much as the buzzword circularity.

IVWhat recycling alone cannot do

In December 2025 Pew and partners updated their study “Breaking the Plastic Wave” (full report as of February 2026). The picture has not improved. In the five years since the first study, by Pew's calculation, 570 million tonnes of plastic pollution have entered land, air and water. Without intervention, production of virgin plastic rises by 52 per cent by 2040, from 450 to 680 million tonnes, twice as fast as the capacity of waste management, which grows by only 26 per cent. The share of uncollected plastic waste rises from 19 to 34 per cent. Pollution overall grows from 130 to 280 million tonnes a year. Microplastics make up 13 per cent of it worldwide in 2025, already 79 per cent in high-income countries according to the press release, and they are growing by more than 50 per cent.

On recycling, the study explicitly says what this essay argues. Mechanical recycling, it says, reduces the strength, flexibility and other properties of plastic with every cycle. That limits the number of possible cycles, and in some cases virgin material has to be added to maintain quality. Recycling alone can therefore only slow the exponential growth of new production, not prevent it. The study also gives a figure: in 2025, 64 per cent of recycled plastic runs through open-loop facilities, that is, into products other than those it came from (benches or asphalt, for example). In the scenario with all measures, that share is to fall to 42 per cent while the closed-loop share rises to 57 per cent.

The “System Transformation” scenario, which uses only existing means, cuts pollution by 83 per cent by 2040. It triples mechanical recycling, but its core is something else: new production falls by 44 per cent compared with the projection. For packaging, two thirds of the reduction comes from reuse systems. Microplastics fall by only 41 per cent, less than anything else. That is the most uncomfortable figure: the part that is hardest to retrieve is also the hardest to reduce.

The first study of 2020 (data from 2016) had already calculated this strategy by strategy: no single strategy brings the flow into the ocean below the 2016 level by 2040. An ambitious expansion of recycling reduced it by 38 per cent at the time, reduction and substitution by 52 per cent. Only 21 per cent of plastic was economically recyclable; with better design it could have been 54 per cent.

The lever “less” does not work by itself either. In Germany the reuse rate for beverage packaging was 34.5 per cent in 2024; the statutory target of 70 per cent continued to be missed. The share of single-use plastic bottles fell in four years from 40.7 to 38.6 per cent.

VThe Antalya figure: 15 per cent of what?

Here comes the question from the subtitle. A target such as “15 per cent circular materials” can be met in several ways. If you count the share of secondary material in the input, every kilo of recovered material counts, regardless of what it becomes and how often it can still be used. If instead you count how much material stays in the loop at the same quality, the figure comes out lower, and you would need data that hardly anyone collects so far.

The German packaging figures show how much the point of measurement alone makes: almost 20 percentage points. Which method the presidency means I have not been able to establish from the available sources; the reports give the figure without a definition. That the target could also be met with downgraded material is my inference from the logic of measurement, not a statement by any source. When it comes to implementation, this definition is exactly what deserves a close look. The figure has not been adopted. The target belongs to the presidency's programme, and the negotiated COP31 closing text was not adopted on Sunday. Whether the 15 per cent will live on in another form is open. The question of the definition remains, and it does not get smaller when the framework is missing.

VIThe treaty stalls, time does not

The place where the problem was supposed to be regulated in a binding way is the UN plastics treaty. The negotiations in Geneva in August 2025 ended without agreement on a text. Since February 2026 Julio Cordano of Chile has chaired the process. His latest working paper is, according to Climate Home News, a step backwards on limiting production. He himself calls it an informal document and not a negotiated text. The next formal round is aimed for late 2026 or early 2027.

That matters, because Pew puts a figure on the delay: five years of postponement put 540 million tonnes more plastic into the environment and cost governments an additional 27 billion dollars a year. The figure is a model value, not a measurement. But the direction is clear: whoever waits pays with a stock that can no longer be retrieved later.

VIICoda: what does not come back

A cycle loses nothing. A cascade loses something at every step.

This is the pattern of this site again. One expects the system to return if only it is sorted properly. Material behaves differently. It has states, and the way back does not follow the same path as the way out. What arrives in the environment as microplastic, no one will ever retrieve. What ends up as fibre in a fleece jacket will hardly become a bottle again.

This is not an argument against recycling. It is an argument for an honest number. A target that measures the share but not the quality gives the cycle a name it has not yet earned.
