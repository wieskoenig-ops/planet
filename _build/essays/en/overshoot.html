As of Sunday, 22 November 2026, afternoon. In the closing plenary the President of COP31 stated that there was no consensus for the closing text, the “Antalya Pact”, and that it was not adopted. How things will proceed was open at the time of writing. This essay refers to that final draft, and to what its failure changes and what it does not.

IA word becomes official

At climate conferences the world rarely changes. Words change. The final COP31 draft contained a new one: it would have recognised for the first time that warming will exceed the 1.5-degree threshold, and it called for faster development of carbon dioxide removal technologies in order to reverse the overshoot later.

That would have been an honest sentence. The mark can no longer be held; experts have been saying so for years. It would also have been a risky sentence, because it contains a word that sounds like a plan: overshoot.

Overshoot means going beyond the target, staying above it for a while and coming back down. A dent in the curve. This essay asks whether the climate sees it that way too.

IIWhat UNEP says, and what it does not

At the beginning of September the UN Environment Programme published a report that brought the word into the debate. Its finding: exceeding 1.5 degrees is considered unavoidable under current policies. Even in the most favourable scenario the peak lies at 1.8 degrees; most others are higher. As early as May, the World Meteorological Organization considered it likely that the average of the years 2026 to 2030 will lie above 1.5 degrees, with a probability of 75 per cent.

As the best remaining option the report names the path overshoot, peak and decline: exceed, reach the peak, fall back.

What matters is what UNEP does not say. The report explicitly does not treat the overshoot as a new target, but as a path that must be kept as small and as short as possible. The higher the peak and the longer it lasts, the greater the risks. UNEP's head Inger Andersen puts it soberly: above 1.5 degrees there are no good outcomes.

The report is not an all-clear. But a negotiating text has a way of turning a path into a word.

IIIWhat comes back, and what does not

If everything succeeds, the global mean temperature can be brought down again. The climate that produced it does not come back with it.

A study by around thirty researchers in Nature in 2024 shows this: even if the temperature falls again after an overshoot, some of the damage done at the peak remains irreversible, such as sea level rise. The patterns of precipitation and extremes are also different afterwards. One does not return to the old world, but to a new one that has the same average temperature.

That is hysteresis, as described in the essay The way back is a different one. The way out and the way back do not lie on top of each other.

The temperature can come back. The climate that produced it cannot.

IVDuration matters

A qualification belongs here, because it makes the text more honest. Not every threshold is a guillotine. A review in Environmental Research Letters (Ritchie et al., 2026) finds that tipping elements with a fast response time, such as warm-water coral reefs, are particularly vulnerable to an overshoot. Slow-responding ones, such as the polar ice sheets, may be less sensitive to a temporary overshoot.

That means: if the time spent above the critical threshold is short enough, some slow elements may still avoid tipping, because they cannot respond fast enough. This does not apply to fast systems, and for the slow ones only as long as the peak stays short. Moreover: the higher the temperature rises and the longer it stays there, the greater the probability that tipping points will be triggered.

We do not know exactly where the thresholds lie. Every year above the mark is therefore a year in which one is betting on time.

VThe bet on removal

The way back requires humans to pull carbon dioxide out of the air on a very large scale. Researchers who calculate overshoot scenarios consider it possible that a precautionary capacity of several hundred gigatonnes net will be needed. In the same breath they warn against using removal up on emissions that could be avoided.

Removal is thus meant as insurance. It becomes dangerous when it becomes an excuse: whoever promises future removal can keep emitting today. That is the same linear fallacy that keeps appearing on this site, the idea of an account on which things can be settled later.

A second look is worthwhile, though. That removal was in the draft was, according to one report, mainly due to pressure from the small island states. For them, overshoot is not jargon but a question of survival. At the same time they demanded that the conference treat the overshoot with full rigour on the basis of the IPCC findings. Reading removal in the text as a mere concession to the fossil industry falls short.

The capture of CO₂ from industry and power plants is a different matter. A global target for it was in Thursday's draft. Climate Home News names Saudi Arabia and Russia as its main supporters; the EU did not want it in the decision. Lead negotiator Chris Bowen defended it with reference to the IPCC and the IEA. Removal from the air and capture at the smokestack are not the same thing, even if negotiating texts like to mention them in one breath.

VIWhat a negotiating text cannot do, and a missing one even less

Monday's first draft still called for an immediate transition away from fossil fuels; by Thursday every mention had been deleted. According to reports, this followed objections from Saudi Arabia and other major oil and gas producers. At the same time there was a dispute about the science itself: to a Saudi-led push to set aside the UN report on overshoot, its authors replied that the review met IPCC standards.

On Sunday, 44 hours after the official end, the text then failed, and not at the point this essay describes. President Kurum stated that there were parties in the room rejecting the text, and no consensus. The EU, Panama and other states objected that the pact did not mention fossil fuels at all. The objection was not directed against recognising the overshoot.

That is the irony. The sentence that 1.5 degrees will be breached has not become official. But that does not make it wrong. The atmosphere learns as little of a text's failure as of its success. It responds to what arrives.

And the text would only ever have named part of the problem. It recognised the overshoot, no longer named the cause, and sped up the repair. Read this way, one sees how a path turns into a word that can be renegotiated. A climate system does not allow itself to be treated that way.

What follows now is open. Whether there will be renegotiation, whether the pact will move to the next conference, or whether Friday's partial decisions will stand alone had not been decided at the time of writing.

VIICoda: fatigue, not fracture

A steel girder that is briefly loaded beyond its limit springs back. It looks as it did before. But micro-cracks have formed in its structure, and they grow with every further load. Engineers call this fatigue. It is invisible until it is not.

That is roughly how one should imagine the overshoot: not as a dent, but as a trace in the material. The way back is possible, but it does not lead through the same door.

How high the peak will be and how long it lasts is not decided in a text. It is decided in the emissions of the coming years. That is the uncomfortable and at the same time reassuring news: how wide the loop becomes is still in our hands.
