IThe wrong question

The AGI debate asks: When will a machine become as intelligent as a human? As if there were a set date on which someone flips the switch.

No neuron ever noticed that it had become part of a mind. There was no moment in which cells turned into consciousness. There was thickening. Feedback. Thresholds that are only called thresholds in hindsight.

Whoever looks for the event in a network is looking in the wrong place.

IIPlanetary cognition

I propose a different term:

Planetary cognition denotes the emergent capacity of the technosphere to capture, store and integrate information globally, to anticipate future states and to produce from them coordinated adjustments that cannot be attributed to any single actor. It does not arise in a moment, but through a gradual thickening of connections and feedback.

The term presupposes neither consciousness nor AGI. Within this frame, AGI would at most be a local manifestation. Planetary cognition would be a property of the whole.

IIIWhere the term comes from

The term is not new. Bruce Clarke uses systems theory to describe Gaia as a self-referential system of planetary cognition that regulates global states without presupposing consciousness. N. Katherine Hayles detaches cognition from consciousness and thinks of it as an integrated frame of organisms, machines and their symbiosis with humans. Adam Frank, David Grinspoon and Sara Walker describe technological intelligence as a planetary transition. Benjamin Bratton speaks of planetary computation.

These approaches refer to the biosphere, to ecology or to infrastructure. I use the term for the technosphere: people, markets, algorithms, data centres, flows of capital. And I do not ask whether it computes, but whether it anticipates.

IVIntelligence as a process

We treat intelligence as a property that a system has. Perhaps it is the process by which a complex system becomes increasingly able to anticipate its own future.

That is more than self-organisation. An ant trail is ordered. A traffic jam is coherent. A hurricane holds itself stable. But none of these systems carries a model of its future within it. They react; they do not anticipate.

What does it mean to anticipate? The theoretical biologist Robert Rosen put it precisely in 1985: an anticipatory system contains a model of itself and its environment that runs ahead of reality, and it changes its state now according to what that model predicts for later. That is the criterion. Not order, not stability, but a present that is steered by an anticipated future.

The brain anticipates. So does an organism. And markets do in their own way: Hayek saw in the price a signal that bundles distributed knowledge no individual possesses. Nobody has an overview of the global supply chain. Even so, it plans ahead, balances, corrects.

What, then, is new? Hayek's price was a signal that people read and translated into action. Today the loops increasingly close without them: sensors measure, models forecast, algorithms act, and the result is measured again, in milliseconds and across the whole globe. What is new is not distributed intelligence. What is new is the speed, density and reach of the feedback, and that within it the human being turns from decision-maker into node.

Markets get things wrong; bubbles are failed forecasts. But the brain errs constantly too. Predictive processing, the theory of Karl Friston, Andy Clark and others, describes perception as continuous prediction plus error correction. Intelligence does not mean never being wrong. It means learning from the error before the next one comes.

VHow one would recognise it

Not directly. But indirectly.

First: anticipation without anyone anticipating. The price of wheat that will only be delivered in a year rises today when a drought begins to show somewhere. Insurers withdraw from regions whose risk no law has yet recognised, and houses there lose value before the first flood arrives. In each of these cases the present changes because a future was modelled. And no one owns this model as a whole. It is spread across traders, analysts, weather services, risk models. That is anticipation in Rosen's sense, without a subject who anticipates.

Second: decisions without a decider. In 2010 the Flash Crash wiped out almost a trillion dollars of market value within minutes. No actor had planned it; none understood it at the time. The system acted. But here lies a limit as well: the Flash Crash was not anticipation but a cascade of reactions, algorithms responding to algorithms. It shows that the system acts. Not that it thinks ahead.

Third: self-reinforcing capital loops. Computing power makes models possible. Models justify investment. Investment finances computing power. Nobody steers this loop, and yet it draws energy, capital and talent towards itself as if it had a goal. On its own that is mere feedback, as with the hurricane. What makes it remarkable is that it feeds on expectations: the investment goes not into what is, but into an anticipated future.

None of this proves anything. The first is a candidate for cognition; the other two are borderline cases: they show a system that acts without anyone acting. Whether it thinks in doing so is decided by anticipation. But all of this is exactly what one would expect if the thesis is right, and not what one expects of a mere tool.

VIThe strongest objection

The brain has a self-model. It knows that it is there.

Does the technosphere have anything comparable? Or is it more an ecosystem than an organism: complex, adaptive, full of feedback, but without a centre?

I do not know, and I think it more honest to leave this open. But there is an uncomfortable possibility: a self-model could be distributed. In analysts, rating agencies, risk models, benchmarks, reports about itself. A system that observes itself without an observer existing.

VIIWhere it fails

If planetary cognition exists, it has a strangely short horizon.

It anticipates the next quarterly figure, the next interest-rate move, the next harvest. It does not anticipate the tipping of the AMOC, the melting of Greenland, the point beyond which the way back is a different one from the way there. The signals are there, in measurement series, models, reports. But they are not translated into prices, or only when it is too late. For hysteresis this system has no organ.

That does not refute the thesis. Brains, too, anticipate selectively: whatever they are rewarded for. So the question is not only whether the technosphere thinks ahead, but which future it anticipates, and whose. A cognition trained on returns sees returns. Whatever cannot be expressed in its currency remains invisible to it.

This is where the idea touches politics. In this picture, politics would be the attempt to inscribe a longer horizon into the larger system: through a price on carbon dioxide, through laws that protect future generations. It is the place where one part of the system can think about timescales that the whole overlooks.

VIIIWhat changes

If this is right, the question shifts.

No longer: When will AGI arrive?
But: What are we already, and how would we recognise it?

Perhaps nothing is coming. Perhaps something has long been here. Not as a machine with consciousness, but as a larger cognitive system whose thinking unfolds on timescales and in patterns that remain invisible to individual people.
