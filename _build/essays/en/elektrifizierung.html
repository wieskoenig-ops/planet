As of Sunday, 22 November 2026, afternoon. The target was part of the COP31 closing text, the “Antalya Pact”. In the closing plenary the President stated that there was no consensus for it and that the text was not adopted. How things will proceed was open at the time of writing. The pledge made by several countries at the leaders' summit on 11 November is a separate matter.

IOne target, two numbers

The COP31 presidency set a target that is easy to remember: 35 by 35. By 2035 electricity is to cover 35 per cent of global final energy consumption. At the summit of heads of state and government on 11 November several countries signed up to the pledge. The target was also in the conference's closing text, until that text was not adopted on Sunday.

From what starting point depends on how you count. The presidency speaks of just over 20 per cent, the Climate Action Tracker of just under 24 per cent in 2023. The difference is not the point. The point is the slope: by the Tracker's calculation, electrification would have to grow almost four times as fast as it has so far. With current policies it would reach only 28 per cent by 2035.

The target is a good one. Clean electrification makes economies less exposed to oil and gas price shocks, and according to the Tracker it is one of the most important levers for keeping the overshoot small and short. In a negotiating process otherwise fond of the conditional, it is also something concrete. The question of this essay is therefore not whether we should electrify, but what lies between the decision and the socket.

IIIt is not the power that is missing, but the line

In its report Electricity 2026 the International Energy Agency describes an age in which electricity demand grows at least two and a half times as fast as total energy demand. The bottleneck is no longer generation. In many regions a lack of grid capacity is holding back the connection of new generation, new storage and new consumers. According to the IEA's estimate, investment in grids must rise by about half by 2030.

And the components are getting scarce. According to an IEA survey of manufacturers, it takes two to three years to procure cables, and up to four years for large transformers. Both are almost twice as long as in 2021. For direct-current cables, which are needed over long distances, waiting times extend beyond five years.

This is where the real problem lies, and it is a problem of time. A data centre, a wave of heat pumps or a boom in electric cars take months to appear. A transformer takes years. Two systems running at different speeds are coupled together, and the fast one runs ahead of the slow one until the slow one becomes the bottleneck.

There is good news too. The IEA notes that considerable capacity can be freed up in the short term by making better use of existing grids, with technology that is cheap and quick to install. That buys time. It does not replace building.

IIICopper: two yardsticks

Almost every form of electrification needs copper: in lines, transformers, motors, heat pumps and electric cars. According to an S&P Global study from January, global demand is set to rise from 28 to 42 million tonnes by 2040, while without new mines output peaks at 33 million tonnes around 2030. That would leave a gap of around 10 million tonnes in 2040.

How urgent that is depends on which yardstick you use. The International Copper Study Group even expects a surplus of 368,000 tonnes of refined copper in 2027. BloombergNEF, by contrast, looks at mine capacity and concludes that 332 billion dollars must flow into new mines to avoid a deficit. Both can be true. In the short term the market is roughly balanced, with a slight surplus or deficit depending on the estimate, and the risk lies in the delay: whoever does not invest now will only notice when it is too late, because a mine does not appear on demand.

IVThe mine beneath the power station

Copper can be recycled better than most materials: after remelting it has the same properties as freshly mined copper. In German refining, around 41 per cent of the metal came from recycling in 2024; Aurubis, Europe's largest recycler, reaches 45 per cent and aims for half by 2030.

That is not enough to close the gap. Even if recycled material more than doubles to about 10 million tonnes by 2040, a shortfall remains by S&P's calculation. Another thought is more interesting: an EU-funded study by Empa concludes that recycling old fossil installations could cover all of the steel and around a third of the copper needed for the energy transition. The raw materials for the new system are, to a good extent, contained in the old one.

A further point calms the tone a little. A trade portal notes that the demand is limited in time. Grids, charging infrastructure and heat pumps are being built across the board for the first time, and once the system is in place, less will be built. The bottleneck is a build-up problem, not a permanent state. That holds, however, only if the build-up succeeds.

VElectricity is not yet an exit

Electrification helps the climate only if the electricity is clean. According to model calculations by the International Renewable Energy Agency (IRENA), reaching 35 per cent by 2035 in line with 1.5 degrees would require at least 78 per cent of electricity from renewable sources. The Heinrich Böll Foundation therefore asks whether the target accelerates the energy transition or is a way of locking in more fossil infrastructure.

The concern is not abstract. As described in the essay on overshoot, the draft closing declaration made no mention of fossil fuels at all. Instead, the text of Sunday morning kept the target of 35 per cent by 2035, referred to an earlier decision on the transition away from oil, gas and coal, and called for faster carbon dioxide removal. The target thus took the place of naming the cause. That is not the same as addressing it.

The other side made the point itself. According to Climate Home News, a coalition of Panama, France, Belgium, Finland, Sweden, Spain, Vanuatu, Côte d'Ivoire and Guatemala demanded that the electrification target be explicitly based on clean power. The text failed because fossil fuels were missing. The 35 per cent itself was not what the dispute was about.

There is also a matter of time, familiar from hysteresis. What is built in lines, power plants and installations over the next few years stays in operation for decades. Infrastructure has a memory. Whoever builds electrification on a fossil base now sets the course for a long time.

VICoda: the gap between decision and line

A target can be agreed in a single night. A transformer takes up to four years.

Between the wish for electricity and the electricity lie copper, steel, permits and years. Whoever means the target seriously has to take the supply chain as seriously as the number.

That the closing text failed takes away the target's formal framework, not its arithmetic. Grids and transformers do not ask for consensus.

This is not a rejection of 35 by 35. It is a reminder that the road there is decided somewhere other than in the plenary hall: in transformer factories, in mines, in the scrapyards of old power stations and in permitting offices. Urgent, but not hopeless: a good share of the raw materials is already in the country, and part of the grid capacity can be raised faster than one might think. What matters is that someone starts while the target is still fresh.
