As of Friday, 27 November 2026. COP31 in Antalya ended on 22 November without a joint closing text. Individual decisions, for instance on adaptation and a just transition, were adopted. The next conference, COP32, takes place in Addis Ababa in November 2027.

IA sentence that comes too soon

„Failure as an opportunity“ is a sentence that almost always comes too soon. It consoles before one has understood what actually failed.

In Antalya a text failed. Not the Paris Agreement, not the decisions already taken on Friday, not the obligations states entered into long ago. What failed was the expectation that around 190 states will agree every year on a common sentence about the direction of travel. That is less than the headlines suggested. And more than the reassurances admit.

The question of this essay is therefore not whether the failure can be talked up. It is this: under what conditions does something follow from a failure? For a failure has no direction of its own. It is a state in which the old direction no longer holds and the new one has not yet been settled.

IICopenhagen

There is a precedent, and it is often cited these days. In 2009 Copenhagen was meant to produce a binding agreement, with targets handed down to states from above. What came out was a short paper that the conference did not adopt but merely „took note of“. It was regarded as a disaster.

Six years later came Paris. Paris was not a better attempt at the same plan, but a different plan. Instead of distributing a global target from above, each state names its own contribution, and at regular intervals the contributions are added up to see whether the sum is enough. What is remarkable is that this architecture was already present in the failed Copenhagen paper: it asked states to submit their own pledges. What was considered a stopgap in Copenhagen became the principle in Paris.

The lesson cuts both ways, though. Paris could be agreed because it binds less. The opportunity of Copenhagen was paid for with bindingness. A failure can expose a better form. But it can also merely show how far ambition has to be lowered before everyone agrees.

IIIWhat Antalya exposes

What did Antalya make visible? Above all, the rule by which decisions are made. At climate conferences decisions are taken by consensus, which gives every state a veto. The text on the conference itself (in German) puts it this way: at the negotiating table, any part can prevent the whole.

That this is so was never decided. The draft rules of procedure from 1995 contain a rule under which a two-thirds majority can decide when no agreement is reached. It was never adopted, blocked mainly by oil states led by Saudi Arabia. Since then consensus has applied, because no one could agree on anything else.

Therein lies a loop familiar from this site. To abolish the consensus rule, you need a consensus. A system protects its own rule by making the rule the condition for changing it. The EU has announced that it will propose a review, and UN climate chief Simon Stiell has held out the prospect of reforms before the next conference. Whether this becomes more than a mandate to look into it is open.

One should not fool oneself here. That a majority decision in Antalya would have produced a stronger text is not established. And a decision that the major producing countries do not support does not burn a single barrel of oil less. Consensus is an obstacle, but it is also the reason everyone is at the table. A reform that removes the one risks the other.

To abolish the consensus rule, you need a consensus.

IVThe coalition alongside

While the common text was being fought over in Antalya, something else was growing alongside it. In April, governments met in Santa Marta, Colombia, for the first conference on transitioning away from fossil fuels, hosted by Colombia and the Netherlands, with 57 governments at the start. They set up a scientific panel and agreed on national roadmaps for the phase-out. In Antalya further states announced such roadmaps; depending on the count there are now around two dozen. The next conference is planned for 2027, hosted by Ireland and Tuvalu.

That is the other model. Not everyone, but those who are willing. Whoever goes ahead does not have to wait for whoever wants least. For the transition away from fossil fuels, which disappeared from the text in Antalya, it is so far the only place where it is negotiated explicitly.

But the weakness showed while the conference was still running. Colombia, which had launched the initiative, has withdrawn. A new government is betting on oil and gas again. A coalition of the willing depends on the will of governments, and governments change. What begins voluntarily can end voluntarily. Whether voluntary pledges actually reduce production and consumption is the open question by which this model must be judged.

VWhen a failure tips

In the language of tipping points one can say more precisely when a failure becomes an opportunity. A well-worn procedure is a stable state. It absorbs disturbances and returns to itself: every weak conference is followed by the next, in the same pattern. A failure is a disturbance. Whether the system then falls back into its old state or passes into a new one does not depend on the disturbance. It depends on whether there is another state that can already carry the load.

Before Paris there was one: the idea of national pledges was in the world before it became the rule. Today there are candidates. The Santa Marta coalition, the national roadmaps, the reform proposals. And, perhaps most important, an economy in which clean energy is already the cheaper option in many regions, with all the bottlenecks described in the essay on electrification. Research on social tipping points, such as the work of Ilona Otto and colleagues, has named such levers: energy generation, financial markets, norms, education. If they take hold, the conference text is no longer the place where the direction is decided. It becomes a confirmation of what is already happening elsewhere.

But there is also the other outcome. A failure that costs nothing makes the next one easier. Trust has a memory too: whoever has once seen that a veto is enough will use it again. As hysteresis shows, the way back does not always lead to where one came from. A failure can turn into the normal state.

VIThe price

Even if everything goes well, this opportunity has a price, and its name is time. From Copenhagen to Paris took six years. The atmosphere does not have six years like those. Every year spent on questions of procedure is a year in which emissions continue and the overshoot becomes higher or longer.

This is where the two clocks meet. The clock of negotiations can afford a failure; it simply resets to the next conference. The climate clock keeps counting. What is a pause for diplomacy is, for the climate, an entirely ordinary year.

VIICoda: the vacancy

A failure is not an opportunity. It is a vacancy.

A vacancy does not stay empty for long. It is filled by whatever is already standing by: by a coalition that goes ahead, by an economy that has long since done the sums, or by the habit of carrying on even without agreement.

Whether Antalya will be remembered like Copenhagen, as a failure from which something new emerged, is not decided in Antalya. It is decided by who fills the vacancy first.
