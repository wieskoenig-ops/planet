IA word changes

In January a UN institute swapped one word for another. The report by the United Nations University (UNU-INWEH) considers “water stress” and “water crisis” too weak. A crisis passes. Instead the report speaks of water bankruptcy: a state in which use has taken out more than comes back in, year after year, and the damage to water systems can no longer be undone.

Not every region is bankrupt, the author Kaveh Madani stresses. But enough critical systems have crossed that threshold.

This is hysteresis in the language of accountants. A state you can return from is called a crisis. One you cannot return from is called bankruptcy.

IIThe savings account

The image the report uses is simple. The running income of water management is rivers, soils and snowfall. The savings are groundwater, glaciers and wetlands. Many societies have not only overspent their annual income but also drawn down their savings.

The World Meteorological Organization describes the same pattern. Its Secretary-General Celeste Saulo points out that the slow parts of the water cycle, meaning groundwater, glaciers and snowpack, act as a buffer against single years of drought. “We are depleting this savings account”, she says. According to the WMO, water storage on land has been declining since the mid-2010s.

The figures for 2025 fit the picture. For the world's rivers it was one of the driest years in more than three decades. Asia and Africa accounted for at least half of all known water-related extreme events in the past five years and for more than 80 per cent of the deaths linked to them.

The treacherous thing about a buffer is that it hides the situation. As long as it is there, you hardly feel the deficit. How little of it is left only shows once it is gone.

IIIThe mountain as a reservoir

On 26 August a section of a Himalayan glacier broke off on the border between Nepal and Tibet. The masses of ice and rock set off a flood. According to Reuters, by mid-September at least 1,300 people had been confirmed dead and more than 5,300 were missing. The cause has not been established. Researchers consider it too early to attribute the collapse directly to warming, even though they do not consider the conditions for it a coincidence.

A few weeks later a report by the consultancy Systemiq and two Himalayan institutions followed. According to the figures reported by Down To Earth, the glaciers are losing mass 65 per cent faster than a decade ago. Most river basins will reach “peak water” by around the middle of the century. Of the region's roughly 40,000 glaciers only 0.1 per cent are monitored regularly, 21 of them on the ground. In India, 189 glacial lakes are considered dangerous, 56 of them especially so.

Here a distinction that often blurs in the coverage. “Peak water” is the point at which runoff stops rising and begins to fall. It is a turning point in a trajectory, not a tipping point in the strict sense, meaning a threshold with self-reinforcement. The report uses the term anyway, without naming a threshold. We use the word more strictly here.

What makes this trajectory dangerous is the time before the turn. As long as more meltwater flows, the river looks like abundance. It is the opposite of an alarm: a sign of wealth that is in truth a drawdown.

IVHysteresis underground

Groundwater is the savings account you cannot see. A study in Nature evaluated 170,000 monitoring wells in 1,693 aquifers in countries that account for about three quarters of global withdrawals. In 71 per cent of the systems the water table is falling. In more than a third it drops faster than 0.1 metres a year, in 12 per cent faster than half a metre. In 30 per cent of cases the decline has accelerated since 2000. It is worst in dry regions with a lot of cropland.

That is where the hysteresis lies. An aquifer fills over very long periods and can be pumped empty in a few decades. On top of that, the damage is not only in the water. The UN report counts compacted aquifers and subsided land in deltas and coastal cities among the irreversible losses. In Mexico City the ground is sinking by about 25 centimetres a year. When the rock compresses as it is pumped out, part of the storage space is gone for good, even if it rains again later.

Agriculture is right in the middle of it. About half of the world's domestic water comes from groundwater, and 40 per cent of irrigation water from aquifers that are steadily emptying. Whoever has to limit withdrawals there limits the harvest.

But it is not fate. The same study also found cases in which aquifers recovered after policy changes and water transfers. That is rare, but it shows that the loop does not have to run to its end in every case.

VWhat we do not yet measure

How well we capture this trend is shown by the yearbook of the Global Climate Action Agenda. In the chapter on cities, infrastructure and water, 16 of 19 indicators lack sufficient data. One qualification: 16 of the 19 indicators are new this year, so what is missing above all is a baseline for comparison. Measurement is only just beginning.

The direction of the available figures is nonetheless clear. The share of safely managed drinking water rose from 68 to 74 per cent. At the same time, the urban population in water-scarce catchments grew from 2.6 to 2.9 billion, and the number of people in floodplains from 1.3 to 1.4 billion. So more water is being supplied, and at the same time more people are exposed to water risks.

The money does not look better. COP30 decided to at least triple adaptation finance by 2035. The need in developing countries could reach 365 billion dollars a year by 2035. Actual adaptation finance, according to the yearbook, fell from 81.5 billion dollars (2022) to 64.1 billion (2024).

In Antalya this was supposed to be sharpened, and it did not happen. The final draft of the closing text named no figure and only provided for a later discussion of a path towards tripling. According to Climate Home News, the island states demanded a concrete figure and a process. Then the text fell in the closing plenary, and with it everything it contained. The planned work programme on mountains and climate, which would have given glacier regions specific attention, was also in that text. Whether it will be rescued separately was open at the time of writing.

VIWhat is not lost

Bankruptcy does not mean the end. The UN report draws an explicit line: bankruptcy has occurred for critical systems, but further deterioration is not inevitable. It names better protection of wetlands, a reform of irrigation, and water rights aligned with the reduced capacity of aquifers. The report calls for a shift from permanent emergency response to “bankruptcy management”: transparent water balances and enforceable limits.

Closer to home, too, it pays to look at the reservoir rather than the rain. A study by NABU and BCG concludes that the water crisis in Germany is not a lack of rain but a loss of natural storage capacity. Heavy rain runs off the surface instead of seeping in. In Austria, groundwater levels have fallen by 30 to 50 centimetres on average over the past ten years. Soils, forests and moors are the part of the account that can be refilled on one's own doorstep.

VIICoda: an account that does not show itself

What filled over centuries can be emptied in years.

A savings account does not show its state in everyday life. You see it only when you need it, and by then the question of how much is left has already been answered.

The pattern is the same as with overshoot: the word comes before the insight. Whoever speaks of a crisis promises a return. Whoever speaks of bankruptcy is talking about managing what remains. That sounds harsher but is more honest, and it leaves more room for action than the term suggests. The threshold has been crossed in some places. How far the loop runs is still being decided.
